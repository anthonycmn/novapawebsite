How to Help a Child With Stage Fright: What Actually Works Backstage

By Tony Cimino-Johnson · Founder & CEO, Northern Virginia Performing Arts

The seahorse would not go on.

Second grader, purple felt, a headpiece somebody's grandmother had glued sequins onto at eleven the night before. Cue was coming. The other sea creatures were already bouncing in a clump in the stage left wing the way seven-year-olds bounce, and this one had gone completely still, both hands wrapped around the leg of a light boom, eyes on the floor. I have seen that stillness maybe three hundred times. It does not look like fear from the outside. It looks like a kid who has decided to become furniture.

Her mother was in the fourth row. Her mother had told me at pickup on Tuesday, "she's been so nervous all week, we've been practicing every night, I keep telling her she's going to be great."

Every night. Going to be great. I did not say anything on Tuesday because there was a line of parents behind her, but I want to say it now, because that sentence, delivered with total love, is most of why the seahorse was holding the light boom.

So here is what I actually know about stage fright in children, from the wings rather than from a pamphlet. Some of it will sound backwards. Bear with me.

Three different things parents call stage fright

When a parent says "my child has stage fright," they usually mean one of three things, and the three need different responses. Most of the advice online treats them as one thing, which is why most of it does not work.

The first is ordinary pre-show arousal. Heart going, hands cold, has to pee, cannot sit down, talking too fast or not at all. This starts an hour or two before and is gone within about ninety seconds of the first laugh. It is not a problem. It is the body doing exactly what a body should do before it goes and does something in front of two hundred people. I have it before every opening night, twenty years in. Do not try to fix this one. Trying to fix it is what turns it into the second thing.

The second is the freeze. The seahorse. A child who was fine at rehearsal, fine at dinner, fine in the car, and then locks up in the wing or, worse, onstage. This is not a decision and it is not stubbornness, no matter how much it looks like both. It is a nervous system that has hit a threshold and pulled the emergency brake. You cannot talk a child out of a freeze. You can only change what their body is doing.

The third is dread. Stomachaches on rehearsal days. Tears on Sunday night about a show that is on Friday. "I don't want to do it anymore" from a kid who begged to sign up. This one starts days out and does not lift after the show, or lifts for an evening and comes back. This is the one where the stage is not really the subject, and I will come back to it at the end, because it deserves its own answer.

Figure out which one you are looking at before you do anything. Most of the time it is the first, and the correct response to the first is almost nothing.

What the adults are doing that makes it worse

I have taught in this county for a long time and I have never once met a parent who was trying to make their child more afraid. And yet. Here is what I watch loving adults do, all season, every season.

Rehearsing at home the week of the show. This feels like help. To a seven-year-old it is a message, and the message is: the grown-ups are worried this will go badly. Children read the emotional temperature of a household far better than they read a script. If your kid knows the material well enough for the director to have put them onstage, they know it. Let them watch TV.

"Don't be nervous." This tells the child that the feeling they are having is the wrong feeling, which now gives them two problems: the nerves, and the fact that they are failing at not having nerves.

"You're going to be great." Also love. Also a raise in the stakes. Great is now the standard, and the child who forgets a line has failed at great. I have watched a fourth grader come off stage after a genuinely lovely scene, sobbing, because she skipped one word and had been told all week she was going to be great.

Watching from the wings. Or the hallway, or the door with the little window in it. If your child can see you during the show, part of their brain stays with you and is not available for the show. The kids who do best are the ones whose parents dropped them at the stage door, said something short, and went and found their seat.

The car ride debrief. "What happened in the second scene?" "Why didn't you sing louder?" "Did you see that you were late on the turn?" I understand the impulse. Please wait a day, and when you do ask, ask what the room felt like, not what went wrong. They know what went wrong. Everybody who has ever performed knows exactly what went wrong within four seconds of it happening.

Why children are less afraid as somebody else

I trained in drama therapy alongside the directing work, and there is one idea from that world I wish every parent had. It is that the role is a container.

A child who is terrified to stand in front of a room as herself will often walk out there without a second thought as a seahorse. Or a Wickersham brother, or Munchkin number four. This is not a trick. It is the oldest thing theatre does. The mask, the costume, the name that is not your name, all of it gives the frightened self somewhere to stand that is one step removed from the exposure. Ask a shy kid to give a speech and they will vanish. Ask the same kid to be the mayor giving the speech, and hand them a hat, and something loosens.

So when your child is scared, do not talk to your child. Talk to the seahorse. "What does the seahorse do when the big fish comes?" "Show me the seahorse's walk." I do this in the wings constantly and it works more often than anything else I have, because it moves the child from the part of the brain that is watching itself into the part that is playing.

The other thing that works is the group. A frozen child alone stays frozen. A frozen child with a castmate's hand in hers, doing the same silly thing at the same time, almost never does. Nervous systems regulate off each other. That is not a metaphor. It is why we warm up in a circle, and why the entrance that goes worst is always the solo entrance and almost never the clump of sea creatures.

What we actually do backstage when a child locks up

Nobody publishes this, so here it is. This is what I do with a seahorse holding a light boom, and it is what I train every stage manager and teaching artist in our building to do.

  1. Get low. Crouch so your face is below theirs. A tall adult leaning over a frozen child is one more thing to be afraid of.
  2. Do not ask what is wrong. They do not know, and now they have to answer a question.
  3. Give the body one job. "Press your feet into the floor as hard as you can." "Squeeze my hands." "Blow out like you are blowing out a birthday candle, slow." Something with muscles and breath, that has an end.
  4. Give them a job that is not about them. "I need you to make sure the crab gets on with you, he is going to forget. Can you do that?" Responsibility for another person is the fastest way out of self-watching I know.
  5. Talk to the character. See above. "Is the seahorse scared of the eel? What does she do about it?"
  6. Send them on with the group, never alone. If the entrance was written as a solo and the kid cannot, a second child goes with them. The audience will never know. The show does not care.
  7. If they still cannot, they do not go. The show survives. A child who is carried onstage against a freeze learns that the stage is a place where their body is overruled, and that lesson takes years to undo. They can go on in the next scene. Or next week. Or next year.

Total time, start to finish, is usually under a minute. The seahorse went on. She went on holding the crab's hand, half a beat late, and nobody in the fourth row could tell you which one of them was late.

What to do on show day, and what to say

Here is the whole parent job, and it is smaller than you think.

Feed them something real. Get them to the building early enough that it stops being new. Hand them to the stage manager at the door and go. Do not linger. At the handoff, say one short true thing and nothing else. "I cannot wait to see it." "I love watching you do this." Not great, not nervous, not remember to. Then find your seat and be visibly, almost boringly, calm, because they will look for you in the house exactly once, and what they need to see is a parent who is not worried.

Afterward, in the lobby, say the true thing again and take them for ice cream. The debrief can wait until tomorrow, and by tomorrow you will find you do not need it.

When it is more than stage fright

I promised to come back to the third thing, the dread, and I want to be careful here, because I am a theatre director and not a clinician and the line matters.

Ordinary stage fright is shaped like the show. It rises toward the performance and it is gone after, usually replaced by a kid who will not stop talking about it. If what you are seeing is shaped differently, pay attention. Dread that starts days out and does not lift. Stomachaches and headaches that cluster on rehearsal days. Refusal that spreads, from the stage to reading aloud in class to birthday parties. A child who loved this last year and has gone quiet about it this year without a reason you can find.

Those patterns are about anxiety, and the stage is just where it happens to be showing up this month. A good theatre program can make the room safer, and I will always try. But the right first call is your pediatrician, not your director. I would rather lose a kid from a cast for a season than have a parent wait on me to fix something that was never mine to fix.

Closing reflection

The seahorse's mother found me in the lobby afterward and said, "she did it, I can't believe she did it, we practiced so much." And I said the thing I always say, which is some version of: she did it because she is brave, and because the crab was holding her hand.

I did not say the part about the practicing. Maybe I should have. I am saying it now, to you, because the season is starting and there are a lot of seahorses out there and a lot of parents rehearsing them at the kitchen table out of nothing but love.

Your child does not need to be more prepared. Your child needs to be less alone in the wing, and less watched, and given a job. The rest is the body doing what bodies do before something matters. Let it.

Questions parents ask

How do I help my child with stage fright before a show?

Stop rehearsing at home the week of the show; it teaches the child that the adults are worried. Feed them, get them there early, hand them to the stage manager and leave. Say something short and true, like "I cannot wait to see it," and nothing about nerves or being great. The most useful thing a parent can do on show day is be visibly, boringly calm.

What should I say to a child who is scared to go on stage?

Not "don't be nervous" and not "you'll be great." Say what is small and true: "Your body is getting ready. That is what it is supposed to do." Then give them one physical thing to do, like pressing their feet into the floor, and one job that is not about them, like making sure the kid next to them gets their cue. Fear is loudest when a child is alone and watching themselves.

When is stage fright in a child something more than nerves?

Normal stage fright is shaped like the show: it rises toward the performance and is gone after. Watch for dread that starts days out and does not lift, stomachaches or headaches on rehearsal days, refusal that spreads to school or reading aloud, or a child who loved this last year and has gone quiet. Those are about anxiety rather than the stage, and they deserve a call to your pediatrician. A director can make the room safer; a director is not a clinician.
