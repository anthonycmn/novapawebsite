Two emails, same morning, the week rehearsals start.

The first: "He has ADHD, and I'm worried he'll be a disruption." The second: "She has anxiety. Please tell me if I'm pushing her." I have received some version of both of those emails every season for twenty years, and for the first few of those years I answered them the way a tired teacher answers: reassurance, a policy, a promise to "keep an eye on him."

Keeping an eye on him was the problem. So I stopped answering the emails and started answering the room.

## The principle: the room is the accommodation

Most of us were trained to think of accommodations as things we do for a child: a seat near the front, extra time, a quiet corner. Those are fine. They are also exhausting, because every one of them is a separate system you run on top of the rehearsal, and by tech week you are running fourteen of them and none of them well.

The better move, the sustainable one, is to build the accommodation into the structure of the room so it runs whether you are watching or not. A rehearsal room that is predictable, that has a job for every body, that treats being wrong as the process, that gives feedback in seconds, and that never leaves a child watched alone, is already doing the work for the anxious kid and the ADHD kid before you have learned either of their names. I laid out those five properties, and the research behind them, in the [canonical piece for parents](https://novapa.org/blog/drama-for-children-with-anxiety-or-adhd-tony-cimino-johnson.html). This is the version for the person who has to build it.

## The system: five things, once, for everyone

1. **Movement before listening.** Physical warm-up first, every rehearsal, no exceptions, including the day you are behind. The eight minutes you "save" by skipping it cost you thirty in a room that cannot settle.
2. **Announce every transition.** "Two more minutes on this scene, then we're on our feet for the storm." Say it to the whole room. The child who needed it will never have to be singled out for it.
3. **Post the plan.** A whiteboard with the day's order, in the same corner, every day. The anxious child reads it and puts the scanner down. The ADHD child reads it and knows what he is running toward. The stage manager reads it and stops asking you.
4. **A job for every body, with motion in it.** Entrance caller. Prop runner. Sound effects. Chair resetter. Not busywork: jobs the show actually needs, that only work if the child is paying attention to everything. The kid who cannot sit in the circle is very often the kid who can run your entrances.
5. **Ritualize the mistake.** When a line goes wrong, the room's response is set in advance: laughter, "again," and a redo. Not silence. Silence is what an anxious child has spent her whole life avoiding, and if your room goes quiet at mistakes, she will stop making choices in it.

Rule: if you find yourself running a separate accommodation for a specific child, ask whether the room could do it for everyone instead. It usually can, and it usually costs less.

## The vignette, short version

The boy from the first email became my assistant stage manager for entrances in week two. By week five he had every actor's blocking memorized and was correcting missed entrances in a stage whisper the whole cast could hear. The girl from the second email learned the entire score in her bedroom before the first rehearsal so nothing could surprise her; by week four her knuckles were a normal color, and by the show she was the funniest thing in it.

Neither child got a special system. They got a room with a system in it.

## The Student Investment pillar, stated plainly

Theatre programs do not grow because the teacher works harder. They grow because more kinds of students find that the room was built with them in mind, and those students bring their families, and those families stay. The anxious child's mother and the ADHD child's mother are two of the most loyal parents a program will ever have, because every other room their child walked into called their child the problem, and yours did not.

That is not a soft benefit. That is enrollment, retention, and a booster club, five years out, brick by brick.

Build the room once. Let it do the work.
