**Title:** The Fifteen Seconds Before the Song
**Subtitle:** What we are actually assessing at a youth audition, and why our rubric should say so out loud.

---

We tell students that auditions are about preparation. Then we build audition
processes that quietly reward something else.

Here is the thing I did not understand for the first several years I cast shows.
By the time a young performer reaches the middle of the floor, most of my
decision-making apparatus has already been running for fifteen seconds. How they
entered. Whether the music was in a binder or on a phone. Whether they gave the
accompanist a tempo or a shrug. Whether they looked at me once, like a person, or
past me, like a firing squad.

None of that was on my rubric. All of it was in my notes.

Let me be clear: that is not a character flaw in directors. It is an unexamined
system. When the criteria that drive a decision live in a director's instincts
instead of on a printed page, students cannot study for them, families invent
folklore to fill the gap, and the kids with a coach or a theatre-literate parent
arrive with an advantage that has nothing to do with talent. That is not a
meritocracy. That is an access problem wearing a rubric's clothes.

So I started writing the invisible criteria down.

**Publish the whole assessment, not the pretty half.** Our audition notices now
say what we score: range and pitch accuracy, whether the cut shows a complete
thought, whether the performer recovers when something goes sideways, and yes,
the walk-in and the thank-you. If it affects casting, it goes on the page.

**Teach the mechanics as curriculum, not as insider knowledge.** How to cut
sixteen bars. How to mark an accompanist's copy. How to transpose into your own
key and why that is not cheating. A student who does not know these things is not
less talented. They are less briefed. Briefing is free.

**Cast the room you want in eleven weeks.** I stopped scoring difficulty a long
time ago. A hard song at seventy percent is a warning; a simple song at a hundred
percent is a professional. What I need to know is who can hold a thought while
music happens around them, and who I would want beside a nervous freshman during
tech.

**Design for the second audition, not the first.** Auditioning is a repeatable
skill, and the only reliable way to build it is repetition without stakes. Every
one of our classes auditions internally. Nobody's season depends on it. By the
time it counts, the room is familiar furniture.

The parent-facing version of this thinking, including the actual song
recommendations by age and the overdone list, is on the NOVAPA blog:
[The Best Audition Songs for Kids, From the Director's Side of the Table](https://novapa.org/blog/best-audition-songs-for-kids.html).

Publish your criteria. All of them. The students who benefit most are exactly the
ones who never had anyone to explain the fifteen seconds.
