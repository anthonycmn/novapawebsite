Wednesday, 4:15 p.m., the second rehearsal of a children's production of *Shrek Jr.* Thirty-one kids on the floor in a circle, and a nine-year-old I'll call Theo is in three of them. He's on his knees behind the ogre, he's under the folding table checking a cable, he's back on the circle line drumming on his shins. His mother's email that morning had one sentence in it I've read a hundred times: "He has ADHD, and I'm worried he'll be a disruption. Please tell me if it's not working."

Four feet from Theo is a girl I'll call Mia, who has not moved since she sat down. Her hands are folded so tightly her knuckles are white. She already knows every word of every song, because she learned them in her room the week before rehearsals started, so that nothing at rehearsal could surprise her. Her mother's email was different: "She has anxiety. She wants to do this so badly and she's terrified. Please tell me if I'm pushing her."

Two emails, two apologies, two families who had already decided their child was the problem.

My students call me Mr. CJ, and here is what I told both of those mothers, and what I'll tell you, because the sentence you typed into the search bar was some version of "will theatre help my anxious kid" or "is drama class a good idea for a child with ADHD," and you deserve a better answer than the ones currently ranking.

Both of those children were in the same room. Neither needed a different room. They needed the same room, built carefully.

## The benefits of drama for children with anxiety: what the research actually says

I trained in drama therapy at the graduate level, so let me be clear about the evidence before I tell you a single story, because most of what you'll read online either skips this part or oversells it.

The most careful summary is a 2023 systematic review in *JCPP Advances* (Keiller and colleagues) that gathered every study it could find on dramatherapy for young people aged eight to eighteen. Fifteen papers. The results were promising: pre-to-post effects ranged from small to very large, and the medium-to-large effects showed up in early-intervention programs run inside schools, which is the closest thing in the literature to what happens in a good after-school class. A 2022 review in the journal *Children* found the same shape: reductions in social anxiety, depressive symptoms, inattention, and aggression, and an increase in assertiveness.

And now the honest part. The evidence base is small. Of the studies that reported numbers, only three were controlled, and none were blinded. The reviewers said so themselves. What the research supports is this: drama, done with intention, reliably moves anxious and inattentive children in the right direction, and nobody has yet run the large trial that would tell us exactly how far.

Put simply: the research and the rehearsal room agree on the direction. The rehearsal room has a twenty-year head start on the why.

## Why it works: distance, containment, and permission to be wrong

Anxiety runs on two fuels: not knowing what is coming, and being watched while it comes. Take away either one and the engine sputters. A rehearsal room, built right, takes away both.

Drama therapists call the first tool **distance**. Mia cannot walk to the center of a circle as Mia and say something. Mia can walk to the center of a circle as the Gingerbread Man and say something, because the eyes are on him, not her, and if the line comes out wrong it was his mistake. The courage she borrows from the character does not go back when she takes the costume off. I wrote about that mechanism for shy children in [a separate piece](https://novapa.org/blog/is-my-child-too-shy-for-drama-class-tony-cimino-johnson.html); for anxious children it is the same door, opened more slowly.

The second tool is **containment**, which is a clinical word for something every good teacher does by instinct: the room runs the same way every time. Same warm-up. Same circle. Same order of events. A rehearsal has a script that tells you, in print, what happens next. For a child whose nervous system spends all day scanning for the surprise, a room with no surprises in it is not boring. It is the first place all week she can put the scanner down.

The third tool is the one the word "rehearsal" gives away. In a rehearsal, being wrong is the job. Nobody gets it right on Wednesday; that is what Wednesday is for. An anxious child has usually built her whole life around not being wrong in public. A room whose entire purpose is to be wrong, together, repeatedly, until it is right, is the opposite of every classroom she has ever sat in. That is not a side benefit. That is the help, in plain clothes.

By week four, Mia's knuckles were a normal color. By the show, she was the Gingerbread Man, and she was funny, and she knew she was funny, and her mother cried in row F. (Yes, I looked.)

## Theatre classes for kids with ADHD: the room where the energy is the point

Now Theo, in three places at once.

Every classroom Theo had ever been in wanted him still. He could not pay that price, so he was always, in some quiet way, in trouble. A rehearsal room does not want him still. A rehearsal room needs thirty-one bodies to move on cue, it needs somebody who notices everything, and it needs energy at 4:15 on a Wednesday when the adults are running out of it.

So in week two I gave Theo a job. Not a punishment job, a real one: assistant stage manager for entrances. He stood in the wing with the cue sheet and sent every character on. He had to know where everyone was, all the time. He had to move, constantly. He had to be loud, on purpose, at the right moment. By week five he had the entire show's blocking memorized, every actor's, not just his own, and when a fairy-tale creature missed an entrance it was Theo, not me, who said "you're on" in a stage whisper the whole cast could hear.

His mother's fear was that he would be a disruption. He turned out to be the infrastructure.

The lesson, stated flatly: a child with ADHD does not need less to do in a theatre class. He needs more to do, with motion in it, with a reason. The research language for this is "inattention decreased." The rehearsal-room language is: give the kid a job that only works if he is paying attention to everything, and then watch him pay attention to everything.

What a good teacher does for an ADHD child, week to week, is mostly craft, not clinical:

- **Movement first.** The physical warm-up comes before anything anyone has to listen to. A child who has just run, stretched, and shouted can sit in a circle for eight minutes. One who hasn't, can't.
- **Transitions announced.** "Two more minutes on this, then we're on our feet." The hardest moment for an ADHD brain is the unannounced switch. Name it and it mostly disappears.
- **Short scenes, fast feedback.** A rehearsal gives a note in seconds, not a grade in three weeks. That speed is why the kid who cannot finish a worksheet can learn forty pages of blocking.
- **A job for the body.** Prop runner, entrance caller, sound effects, the one who resets the chairs. Every job I have named has motion in it. That is not an accident.
- **Interest is the engine.** The ADHD brain locks on to what it cares about with a force that looks, from the outside, like a superpower. A show a child cares about is the strongest magnet I know.

## What a rehearsal room does that a classroom can't

Here is why the anxious child and the ADHD child, who look like opposites in a doorway, thrive in the same room. The room does five things at once, and each child takes the one they need.

**Predictability** | The same structure every week, and a script that says what happens next. (Mia's tool.)

**A job for every body** | Nobody sits and waits; everyone has a role, a cue, and a reason to move. (Theo's tool.)

**Being wrong is the process** | Rehearsal exists to fix mistakes together. Nobody is graded on Wednesday. (Both.)

**Feedback in seconds** | A note, a redo, applause, all inside a minute. (Theo's tool, and Mia's proof.)

**The group carries you** | Thirty-one kids in a storm scene means no one is watched alone. (Mia's tool, and Theo's team.)

Rule: if a class cannot show you at least four of those five in the first session you observe, it is a class that happens to have anxious and ADHD kids in it, not a class built for them.

## What to look for in a drama class for an anxious or ADHD child

Here in Loudoun County, and across the DC metro, there are more children's drama programs than there were five years ago, so you need a way to tell them apart before you pay. Price will not do it. A weekly children's theatre class in Leesburg, Ashburn, or Fairfax runs roughly $80 to $120 a month; ours at [Northern Virginia Performing Arts](https://novapa.org/classes.html) is $90, with the [first class free](https://novapa.org/free-class/) so a nervous child's first session has nothing at stake, including money. What you are paying for is the room, and the room is the teacher.

Reflective considerations, from the doorway:

- Does the class open with movement and close with the same ritual every week (a bow, a circle, a cheer)?
- Can a child participate without speaking in the first few weeks, and does the teacher call that participation out loud?
- Does the teacher announce transitions before they happen?
- Are there jobs in the room that aren't roles: props, cues, sound, resetting the space?
- How does the teacher respond when a child gets a line wrong? (You want laughter and a redo. You do not want silence.)
- When you say "anxiety" or "ADHD," does the teacher ask what works for your child, or start explaining their policy?

If two or more of those make you wince, keep looking. It is not your child. It is the room.

## What to tell the teacher, and what to tell your child

Parents agonize over whether to disclose a diagnosis. Let me take that weight off you: the diagnosis is yours to share or not. What the teacher needs is not the label. It is the manual.

**Not this:** He has ADHD, I'm so sorry in advance, just let me know if he's a problem.

**This:** He does best with a job and a heads-up before things change. He'll be your most reliable kid if he has something to run.

**Not this:** She has anxiety, please don't make her do anything.

**This:** She likes to know what's coming. If you can tell her the plan for the day, she'll do all of it. Please don't single her out to coax her; she'll come on her own.

And to your child, on the drive:

**Not this:** Just try to sit still tonight. Just try not to worry.

**This:** Your only job tonight is to find out what happens in the warm-up. Tell me in the car.

One job. Small. Concrete. Both of these children do beautifully with one job, and both fall apart when the job is "be different than you are."

## When drama class is not the answer

Let me be clear, because the studio blogs will not say it: a drama class is a place, not a clinic. It supports treatment. It does not replace it.

If your child's anxiety is producing real physical distress about ordinary life (sleep trouble, stomachaches, refusals that hold for weeks across everything, not just this), the first call is to your pediatrician, and the class comes after, or alongside, never instead. If your child with ADHD is midway through a medication change, tell the teacher; a rehearsal room is a wonderful place to be, and a hard place to be during the week the dose is wrong. And if you have given a class three honest weeks and nothing in the room is being built for your child, try a different room before you conclude anything about your child. Teachers are not interchangeable. The right one is comfortable with motion and comfortable with silence, and not everyone in theatre is both.

I have written separately about [stage fright and audition anxiety](https://novapa.org/blog/how-to-help-a-child-with-stage-fright-tony-cimino-johnson.html), a narrower problem with its own tools. For a child who is not ready for a weekly commitment, a [short day camp](https://novapa.org/day-camps.html) is a lower-stakes way to find out what the room feels like.

## Closing reflection

Closing night of *Shrek Jr.* Theo in the wing with his cue sheet, sending the Gingerbread Man on. Mia walking into the light as somebody else, and then, at the bow, as herself, which she managed, and which was the whole point.

Two mothers wrote me two apologies in week one. Neither of them owed me one. Their children were never the problem. Every room those kids had walked into before mine was built for a child who sits still and isn't afraid, and their children were neither, so the rooms called them the problem, and the parents believed the rooms.

Build the room right and you find out what the research already suspects: anxiety and ADHD are not obstacles to theatre. In a rehearsal, they are very often the gift. The kid who notices everything runs your entrances. The kid who needs to know what's coming learns the whole show first.

That is the work. That is the room. Bring them to the doorway.

Inspiritu,  
Mr. CJ
