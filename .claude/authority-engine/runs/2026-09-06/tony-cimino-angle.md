**Title:** The Fourteen-Day Musical: What Running a Production Camp Teaches You About Theatre Leadership

**Body:**

Every July I stand in an empty rehearsal room on a Monday morning knowing that in fourteen days, a company of five-to-fifteen-year-olds will open a complete licensed musical for a paying public. No semester. No spring break to recover in. Fourteen days.

The first time I built that schedule, I assumed the constraint was the enemy. I was wrong. The constraint is the curriculum.

A two-week production camp is theatre leadership with the excuses removed. There is no time to over-rehearse Act One while Act Two starves, a failure pattern I have watched sink full-semester productions. There is no time for ego, mine included. Every hour is assigned before the first child walks in: belonging work first, casting inside forty-eight hours, music before staging, runs before polish, and a hard rule that the company goes home rested on the eve of opening.

Three principles transfer directly to any program, school-year or summer:

**Belonging before performing.** The first two days buy the next twelve. A child who feels safe on Tuesday takes direction on Thursday. My drama therapy training says this is the mechanism; twenty years of camps say it works.

**The calendar is the director.** When the schedule makes the decisions, the adults stop negotiating and start teaching. Predictability builds trust, in a faculty and in a nine-year-old.

**Process over product, even at speed.** The show opens either way. The question is whether the children were built up or used up on the way there.

I wrote a longer, parent-facing guide to choosing a summer theatre camp, including the money questions nobody in Northern Virginia likes to answer, on the NOVAPA blog: [Summer Theatre Camps in Northern Virginia: A Director's Honest Guide](https://novapa.org/blog/summer-theatre-camps-northern-virginia.html). The Playbook chapter on production management covers the full dress-week system.
