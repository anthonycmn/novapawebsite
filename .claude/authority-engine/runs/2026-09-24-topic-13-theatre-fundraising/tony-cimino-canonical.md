The first budget I was ever handed for a theatre program was five thousand dollars.

Not five thousand for a show. Five thousand for a year: scripts, rights, lumber, paint, costumes, the microphones that were already broken when I got there. I remember doing the arithmetic in a classroom that was not finished yet, as a roving teacher pushing a cart, and arriving at the obvious conclusion. We were going to have to raise money.

So we did what everyone does. Catalogs. A car wash. The discount card nobody in the building wanted to buy twice. And we raised a bit, and I spent my Saturdays on it, and the next September the budget was still five thousand dollars.

That program eventually ran on something closer to three quarters of a million. And I want to be honest about what got it there, because it was not the car wash, and it was not working harder.

It was giving up on fundraising.

## Fundraising is not revenue, and the difference is the whole game

Here is the distinction I wish someone had drawn for me in that unfinished classroom.

**Fundraising** is asking people for money and giving them something incidental in return. Cookie dough. A clean car. A raffle ticket. The money arrives once, it arrives late, and it arrives with a vendor's cut taken out of it. Then next year you start again from zero, because a fundraiser builds nothing. It is a bucket, and you refill it by hand.

**Revenue** is money the program earns by doing the thing it already does. Tickets. Programs. Concessions. Sponsorship. Classes and camps that run in the building you already have. It arrives on a schedule, it scales with the work, and — this is the part that matters — it is *the same activity twice*. You were going to put on the show anyway.

Put simply: fundraising is a second job you do on top of teaching theatre. Revenue is teaching theatre, priced correctly.

Every listicle you have already read on this topic is a list of second jobs. Fifteen of them, usually, written by a company that takes a percentage of the pasta.

## The math nobody runs

Before you agree to any fundraiser ever again, run this. It takes two minutes and it will end most of the arguments in your booster meeting.

Take the net profit, after the vendor's cut, after what you spent on supplies. Then count every hour it costs *you* — not the volunteers, you — including the emails, the collecting of envelopes, the counting, the chasing of the eleven families who did not turn theirs in, and the delivery day where you sort frozen food in a hallway.

Divide. That is your hourly rate.

**Rule: if a fundraiser returns less per teacher-hour than the work it displaced, it is not a fundraiser. It is a transfer of your life into the program's bank account, and the exchange rate is terrible.**

Do it honestly and most catalog drives come out somewhere south of minimum wage, for the one person in the building who is least replaceable. Meanwhile the fall musical, according to the programs I have compared notes with, commonly nets four to six thousand dollars in box office. One show. No envelopes.

That comparison is the entire argument, and once you have seen it you cannot unsee it.

## The four levers that actually moved the number

None of these are clever. That is the point. Clever does not survive a tech week.

**One: price the ticket like you believe in the work.** Most school programs underprice out of guilt, and then run three fundraisers to cover the gap the guilt created. You are asking a family for twelve dollars and then asking them for eighteen more in cookie dough two weeks later. Charge properly once. Build in the comp structure and the quiet fund for families who need it, and say so publicly so nobody is embarrassed. Dignity is cheaper than a second ask.

**Two: sell the room, not just the seat.** Programs, sponsor pages, a sponsored seat with a nameplate, a business's logo on the back of the shirt. Local businesses have marketing budgets and they are genuinely trying to spend them in the community. Nobody on your booster board has ever been fired for asking the orthodontist. This is not manipulation; this is business, and the ethics valve is that every dollar goes back into students' hands and the books stay open.

**Three: use the building when it is dark.** Your theatre sits empty most of the summer and most of every break. A camp, a workshop weekend, a one-day intensive — programs that run in space you already hold, taught by people you already trust, paid for by families who are already looking for exactly this. This is the single biggest lever on the list and the one most programs never touch, because it looks like more work. Built right, with real staff and real pay, it is the thing that stops being more work.

**Four: get legitimate about where the money lives.** This is unglamorous and it is load-bearing. A program with a clean account structure, a real budget document, and an administrator who understands the numbers gets treated like a department. A program with a shoebox and a Venmo gets treated like a club, and clubs get cut. Structural legitimacy is not paperwork for its own sake. It is what makes the money you raise *stay* raised.

## What to do about the administrator

Every plan above dies without administrative alignment, so name it early rather than discovering it in April.

Bring your principal a one-page document, once a year, before you need anything: what the program costs, what it earns, what it returns to the school in enrollment and community standing, and what you are asking for. Not a complaint. A briefing.

I have watched the same request get refused in a hallway in March and approved in a meeting in September, with identical numbers. The difference was not the money. The difference was that one version arrived as a crisis and the other arrived as a plan, and administrators fund plans.

## The fundraisers still worth running

I am not against all of them. Three survive the math:

- **Anything that happens during a performance you are already running.** Concessions, merchandise, the program ad section, a raffle at intermission. Marginal cost near zero, because the audience is already in the building and you were already there.
- **Anything a booster parent owns end to end.** If a volunteer runs it, collects it, counts it, and hands you a cheque, the hourly-rate math changes completely. Your job is to say thank you and to never, ever take the task back when it wobbles.
- **A single annual ask, done well.** One honest letter, once a year, to the people who already love the program. No products, no vendor cut, no Saturday. It outperforms most of the catalog drives I have seen, and it costs an afternoon.

Notice what all three have in common. None of them adds a weekend.

## Time sustainability is the real constraint

Here is the thing the fundraising listicles never say, because they are not written by anyone who has to teach five classes the next morning.

Your program does not fail because it runs out of money. It fails because you run out. Money problems are survivable and recoverable; a teacher who leaves in year four is not, and the program they built usually goes with them. Every fundraiser you agree to is a withdrawal from the same account your rehearsals come out of, and that account does not have a bake sale.

So the question is never "could this raise money." Almost anything could raise money. The question is: **is this the best possible use of the scarcest resource in the building, which is you?**

Ask it every time. The answer will surprise you, and it will usually be no.

## Closing reflection

I think about that five thousand dollars often, and about the version of me doing sums on a cart in a corridor.

He was not wrong that the program needed money. He was wrong about where money comes from. He thought it came from asking, and it came from building: from work priced honestly, from a building used properly, from a season designed so that the thing we loved doing paid for the next thing we loved doing.

The students never saw the spreadsheet. What they saw was that the microphones worked, that the set got built, that nobody had to sell anything to their grandmother, and that the adult in the room was still standing in year ten.

That is what financial stability actually buys. Not abundance. Predictability. And predictability is the thing that lets you spend your attention on the only part of this job that was ever the point, which is the young people in the room.

Build the system. Then go teach.
