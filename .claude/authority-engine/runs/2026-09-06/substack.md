**Title:** The Hoodie Comes Off
**Subtitle:** What fourteen days of summer theatre actually does to a child, and how to tell the camps that know it from the ones that don't.

**Body:**

Every camp session, there is a child who arrives on the first Monday and will not take the hoodie off. Hood up, arms crossed, standing near the door the way you stand near the exit of a party you did not want to attend.

Twelve days later, that same child bows.

I have run summer production camps long enough to stop calling that arc a surprise. It is the design. And this week I wrote down, in one place, everything I tell Northern Virginia parents who ask me how to choose a theatre camp: what the market actually costs (with real numbers, because I publish prices and most programs will not), the difference between a production camp and a hobby camp and single-day camps, the five questions that expose expensive babysitting, and why the ages 9–12 sessions fill first across the whole region.

The short version is this. A real theatre camp is not activities. It is a production schedule scaled to a young company: belonging before performing, casting inside forty-eight hours, music before staging, a real audience at the end. A child who builds a show in fourteen days learns that big scary things become doable when a room full of people does them together, one rehearsal at a time.

They will not remember every lyric by Christmas.
They will remember that the hoodie came off.
They will remember that they were seen.

The full guide is here: [Summer Theatre Camps in Northern Virginia: A Director's Honest Guide](https://novapa.org/blog/summer-theatre-camps-northern-virginia.html)
