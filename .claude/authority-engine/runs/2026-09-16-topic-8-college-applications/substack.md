Every October a parent waits until the meeting is over, until the other families have filed out, and then asks me the real question. It is never about the show. It is some version of this: "She loves it. But does theatre actually look good on college applications, or should she be doing something more serious?"

I have been asked that question, in that hallway voice, for twenty years. And I have spent most of my career on the other side of it: writing the recommendation letters, building a program where theatre carried college credit on the transcript, and sitting with seniors and their lists in the weeks before the deadlines.

So here is the honest answer, including the parts that usually get left out.

## Does theatre look good on college applications? The short answer

Yes. And not for the reason most families expect.

Theatre does not earn a bonus point for being creative, and colleges do not keep a list of approved activities with drama near the top. What an admissions reader is actually looking for in an activities list is evidence: that a student stuck with something, grew inside it, took on responsibility that other people depended on, and produced something real. Theatre is not special because it is theatre. It is special because it produces that evidence more reliably than almost anything else a teenager can do after school.

Put simply: theatre is not the credential. Theatre is the record.

## What admissions officers actually read in an activities list

The Common Application gives a student ten activity slots and 150 characters to describe each one. A reader at a selective school spends a few minutes on the whole file. They are not tallying activities. They are reading for a handful of things, and every honest admissions officer I have ever spoken with names the same ones.

**Depth over breadth.** Four years of one thing, done increasingly well, beats one year each of eight things. A reader can tell the difference between a student who joined and a student who stayed.

**Escalating responsibility.** Did the student's role grow? Ensemble to featured to lead. Crew to assistant stage manager to stage manager. Member to officer to the person who ran the thing when the adult was out.

**Impact on other people.** Did anyone depend on this student? Did they teach, lead, organize, or build something that outlasted them?

**Initiative.** Did they start anything, or only participate in what already existed?

Notice what is not on that list. The size of the role is not on it. Whether the activity matches the intended major is not on it. And "creative" is not on it either, which is why the usual internet answer, "colleges like theatre because it shows creativity," is true but not useful. Nobody was ever admitted for being creative. They were admitted for what they did with it.

## Theatre as an extracurricular: what it proves that a club cannot

Let me be clear about why theatre produces that evidence so naturally, because this is the part I wish I had known at sixteen.

Opening night does not move. A club can postpone its meeting. A production cannot postpone the audience, and a student who has lived through a tech week understands deadlines in a way that no assignment with an extension policy can teach.

The work is public. A test score is private. A performance, a lighting design, a costume plot, a set built over six weekends, happens in front of two hundred people who paid to be there. Students in theatre learn to be accountable to a room, not a rubric.

The work is collaborative across skills. In one rehearsal room you will find the kid who sings, the kid who welds, the kid who runs a spreadsheet, the kid who paints, the kid who calls cues, and the kid who can calm down the kid who sings. Nobody gets to do it alone. That is not a slogan; it is a scheduling fact.

And the hours are real. A student in a mainstage production is giving ten to fifteen hours a week in season, on top of everything else. Honestly counted on the activities list, that number tells a reader this student can carry a load.

I built a high school theatre program from a plate of grocery-store cookies and twelve students to a department where, some years, between fifteen and twenty percent of the school was enrolled in a theatre class. My students called me Mr. CJ. Most of them did not go on to major in theatre. Their theatre years went with them on the page anyway, because that is where the growing had happened.

## Theatre for the future engineer, nurse, or lawyer

Here is the worry underneath the hallway question: that theatre only "counts" if you plan to do theatre, and that a future biology major is wasting slots on something the reader will dismiss.

It is the opposite. A biology applicant with four years of theatre is more interesting to a reader than a biology applicant with four years of biology club, because the theatre years are doing a different job on the page. They show the student can speak, present, collaborate, hold a deadline, and work in a room full of people unlike themselves. Medicine, law, engineering, business: every one of them is a public, collaborative profession that runs on exactly those skills.

I have written letters for students headed to conservatories and for students headed to nursing school, service academies, and computer science programs, and I have never once softened the theatre paragraph for the non-theatre kid. It is usually the strongest paragraph I have.

There is also a version of theatre that is literally college. During my years at Rock Ridge High School I developed Virginia's first Dual Enrollment Theatre Program, in partnership with Richard Bland College of William & Mary, so that high school students could earn college credit in theatre while still in high school; a $32,000 grant from the Jack Kent Cooke Foundation later expanded it into stagecraft. That mattered to the theatre kids. It mattered just as much to the kids who were never going to major in it: they walked into college with transferable credits, earned in a room where they were already happy. Nobody had to choose between the thing they loved and the thing that "counted." That is the point of building a system instead of asking students to make do.

## How to describe theatre on the Common App

This is where most applications lose the value they earned. The student did four years of real work and then summarized it in five words. A reader cannot give credit for what is not on the page.

Here is the shape I coach students toward, whether they were a lead, a swing, a crew member, or the kid who ran the box office:

**Not this:** Drama Club member, 4 years. Acted in school plays and musicals.

**This:** Performer & student leader, 4 yrs, 9 productions. Lead in 3; taught weekly workshop for 20 middle schoolers; co-ran $6K fundraiser; 12 hrs/wk in season.

**Not this:** Tech crew, 3 years.

**This:** Stage manager, 3 mainstage shows (crew to SM by junior yr). Called 400+ cues, led crew of 14, built the rehearsal-report system the program still uses.

The pattern: name the responsibility, not the club. Count what can be counted, honestly, including hours. Name what grew. Name what you built or taught, because those are the words a reader has been trained to look for, and because an ensemble member who taught a workshop for younger kids has more to say than a lead who only performed.

Two more places theatre belongs on the application. The honors section, if the student earned any: a district or state festival award, a Cappies nomination, Thespian honors, all-county ensemble. And the essay, if, and only if, there is a real story with theatre in it. The essay is not the place to list productions. It is the place to tell a reader what the student learned when the set fell over on opening night and the show went on anyway.

## The recommendation letter your theatre teacher can write

Most students ask their AP teachers for recommendation letters and never think to ask the one adult who has watched them fail, recover, lead, and grow in public for four years.

A theatre teacher's letter is a different document. An English teacher can say a student writes well. I can say that when the lead lost her voice on a Thursday, this student learned the track overnight, went on Friday, and never told anyone how scared she was. I can say that this student stayed after strike to sweep when nobody was watching. Those are the stories a reader remembers, and the rest of the file cannot provide them.

Ask early, in September of senior year. Give the teacher a short list of the moments you want them to remember. And if a school allows an additional recommendation beyond the required ones, this is where it should come from.

## When theatre is the major: what changes

Everything above is true for the student who wants to be a doctor. For the student who wants to be a performer, one thing changes: the activities list stops being the main event.

A BFA acting or musical theatre program admits by audition, and increasingly by prescreen video first. The transcript still has to clear the university's bar, but the decision is made in a room, or on a screen, by faculty watching a monologue and sixteen bars. That is a different process with its own calendar, and the students I work with in [college audition coaching](https://novapa.org/coaching.html) start it a full year before the applications open. If your child is in that lane, the question is no longer whether theatre looks good. It is whether the audition is ready, and that is a separate guide I will write on its own.

For everyone else, which is most theatre kids, the activities list is the main event, and theatre, described well, wins it.

## What I tell the senior thinking about dropping theatre for another AP

Every spring a junior sits across from me with a schedule request form and says some version of: "I love it, but I think I should take AP Statistics instead next year. It will look better."

I do not argue. I ask them to count. Four APs are already on the transcript. A fifth is a duplicate; it tells a reader something they already know. The fourth year of theatre is not a duplicate. It is the year the record completes: the leadership role that only comes with being a senior, the underclassmen who learn from you, the letter from a teacher who has known you since you were fourteen. The transcript has plenty of rigor on it. What it needs is a through-line.

Sometimes the student takes the AP anyway, because they want the class for its own sake, and that is a good reason and I say so. But if the only reason is that it will look better, it usually will not, and the record they have been building for three years is worth finishing.

## Closing reflection

Back to the hallway, and the parent waiting until the room clears.

What she is really asking is whether the thing her daughter loves is also the thing that will be taken seriously, and whether she has to choose. She does not. Theatre looks good on a college application for the same reason it looks good on a life: it is four years of showing up, growing, being depended on, and making something real in front of people. Described honestly, that is the strongest line on the page.

Let her keep it. Then help her count it.
