**Title:** Can I Just Sing the One I Sing in the Car?
**Subtitle:** What directors are actually listening for at a child's audition, and why the perfect song is almost never the impressive one.

**Body:**

A nine-year-old walked to the middle of the floor at one of our auditions, handed the accompanist a phone with a lyric video playing on it, planted her feet, and began thirty-two bars of a song written for a grown woman in a key nobody could have sung.

She got through eleven of them.

Then she stopped, looked at me, and asked: "Can I just sing the one I sing in the car?"

She sang it. It was ordinary. It was in her range. She meant every word. She was cast that night.

I have been behind that table for more than twenty years, and I have read a lot of lists of the best audition songs for kids. Almost all of them sort songs by how impressive they sound. Directors do not sort them that way, and the gap between those two sorting rules is where thousands of families lose a season.

Here is what is actually being assessed in the first fifteen seconds. Where the voice lives, low and high, comfortably. Whether a child can hold pitch and tempo against a piano, which is a genuinely different skill than singing alone. Whether they mean anything by the words. And what they do in the half second after something goes wrong, because that half second is what tech week is made of.

Difficulty is not on the list. A hard song at seventy percent reads as a warning. A simple song at a hundred percent reads as a professional.

The part I want to argue with the internet about is "overdone." Yes, I hear "Tomorrow" and "Castle on a Cloud" and "Let It Go" and "Naughty" more often than any human should. No, that is not a disqualifier at a youth audition. An overdone song sung beautifully beats an obscure song sung badly, every single time. The real cost of a famous song is comparison: your child now shares the room with a performance they did not invite. If they can own it, let them sing it. If they cannot, trade sideways to something with the same feeling and the same range and no ghost attached.

I wrote the whole thing down this week for parents: what we listen for, the overdone list with honest context, actual song recommendations for ages six to nine and ten to thirteen, the four questions that settle the choice, and how to cut sixteen bars without wrecking the song.

[The Best Audition Songs for Kids, From the Director's Side of the Table](https://novapa.org/blog/best-audition-songs-for-kids.html)

One last thing, and it is the part I care about most. After the audition, most of us ask our kids "how did it go." That question teaches a child that the audition was a test they might have failed. Ask what the room was like. Ask who else was there. Ask whether it was fun.

They will not remember which song they sang for their first audition.
They will remember whether they were allowed to sound like themselves.
