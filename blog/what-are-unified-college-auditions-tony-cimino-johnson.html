What Are Unified Auditions? A Plain-English Guide for Families

By Tony Cimino-Johnson · Founder & CEO, Northern Virginia Performing Arts

A ballroom in a conference center, seven in the morning, and the carpet is the color of every conference center carpet there has ever been. Tables going up along three walls, one per school, a paper sign taped to each. Coffee urns that will be empty by nine. Somewhere down the hall a seventeen year old is warming up in a stairwell, because the stairwell has the best acoustics in any building ever constructed.

I have set that room up. My students call me Mr. CJ, and along with running NOVAPA I run a regional college audition event in Leesburg, which means I have spent a lot of hours on the other side of those tables: assigning rooms, feeding reps, watching a parent hover by the door with a garment bag over one arm and no idea what happens next.

And what I hear from that doorway, every single year, is some version of the same sentence.

"So we have to go to Chicago in February?"

Let me be clear: no, you probably do not. But I understand completely why you think you do, and the reason is that the word itself is a mess.

What "unifieds" actually means

The confusion is not your fault. One word is doing three jobs.

  • The National Unified Auditions | The original. A loose coalition of roughly twenty-five to thirty BFA theatre and musical theatre programs that agree to hold auditions in the same three cities each winter: New York, Chicago, and Los Angeles. Chicago is the biggest and runs the most days; Los Angeles is the smallest. This is the thing people mean when they say "Unifieds" with a capital U, and the member list lives at unifiedauditions.com.
  • Regional unifieds | Independent events that use the same word because it describes the same idea: many schools, one building, one weekend. Pittsburgh, Utah, Western New York, and the one I run in Virginia are all in this category. They are not run by the coalition, they do not share a school list, and they each work differently.
  • "United Auditions" | A different event entirely, with a different name that sounds almost identical. Families mix these up constantly.

So when a school's website says "we will be at Unifieds," that sentence is incomplete until you know which one, and every school publishes its own list. Put simply: there is no single official event that every program attends and every serious applicant must get to. That event does not exist. What exists is a scattered set of opportunities, and your job is to pick the ones that overlap with your child's actual list.

What actually happens in that room

Here is the part almost nobody writes down, because almost nobody writing about this has worked the event.

Each school gets a space. Sometimes a hotel suite with the bed taken out, sometimes a classroom, sometimes a corner of a ballroom with pipe and drape. One or two faculty sit behind a table. Your child comes in, hands over a headshot and résumé, says their name and what they are singing, and does sixteen or thirty-two bars and a monologue. It takes about five minutes.

Five minutes. That is the whole thing.

Then there is the dance call, which is usually separate and much bigger: forty kids learning a combination in a room with mirrors, schools watching from the side. And there are information sessions, which families skip because they feel optional and which are, in my honest opinion from the other side, one of the most useful hours available to you.

What the reps are doing in those five minutes is less dramatic than you fear. They are not deciding your child's future. They are making a first sort: is this person trainable, are they right for what this program does, did they choose material that fits them. A rep who liked your child writes something down and calls them back on campus or asks for more. A rep who did not is not rejecting your child as a human being. They have a freshman class of sixteen and they have seen four hundred people since Thursday.

Which brings me to the thing I most want parents to hear: this process is a matching problem, not a ranking. A no from a program that trains heavy classical voice for a kid who belts contemporary pop is not a judgment of talent. It is two lists failing to overlap.

Prescreens come first, and they surprise everyone

Most musical theatre programs now require a prescreen: recorded songs, a monologue, sometimes a dance reel, submitted through a platform (usually Acceptd) in the fall, months before anyone stands in a ballroom. The schools watch those and decide who gets a live audition slot at all.

This reorders the whole year. The panic most families feel in January is misplaced; the real deadline was October. If your child is a junior right now, the prescreen calendar is the thing to build backward from, not the Unifieds dates.

And a live audition slot is generally something you are invited to. You do not simply show up to the coalition's event and audition for thirty schools. Walk-in slots exist at some programs, they are limited, and the lines for them are the least fun part of the weekend.

Do you have to go? The honest rule

No. And here is the arithmetic I would use if it were my kid.

Rule: count how many schools on your child's actual list will be in one building on one weekend. Three or more, go. One or two, audition on campus instead.

A campus audition is a better experience almost every time. Your child sees the building they would live in, meets students, gets a longer slot and a less frantic room. The only real advantages of a unified event are density and cost per school, and if the density is not there, you are paying travel money for an inferior version of something you could do better on campus.

That rule applies to my own event too. If the school your child cares most about is not coming to Leesburg, I would rather say so than take your registration.

What it actually costs

Nobody itemizes this, so here it is, honestly. Published figures vary, and the widely cited total for a full BFA audition season is upwards of $5,000. That number is real, and it is mostly not audition fees.

  • Application and audition fees | Per school, and they stack. Fifteen schools adds up quickly before anyone has bought a plane ticket.
  • Prescreen production | Accompanist, a room, someone competent behind a camera. This is where families either spend money or spend a weekend learning to do it themselves.
  • Travel | The largest line by far. Flights and a hotel in New York or Chicago in midwinter, for two people, for three or four nights.
  • Coaching | Optional, genuinely useful, and wildly variable in price. I do this work and I still tell families it is the line to cut first if something has to go.

This is the honest case for a regional event, and I will state my interest plainly since I run one: driving ninety minutes and sleeping at home is a different financial event than flying to Chicago. That is the whole pitch. It is a real advantage and it is not the only thing that matters.

What I would tell you if you were standing in my doorway

Build the school list first, then the audition calendar. Never the other way around. The list is the strategy; unifieds are only logistics.

Go to the information sessions. Your child is exhausted and wants to go back to the room. Go anyway.

Let your child pick their own material, with help. Faculty can tell within eight bars when a song was chosen by an adult who wanted to impress them.

Plan the day around food and a quiet hour. I have watched more auditions go sideways from a skipped lunch than from a wrong song.

And decide, out loud and in advance, what you will say in the car afterward. "How did it go?" is a harder question than it sounds when your child does not know and cannot know. "I'm proud of you, what do you want for dinner" is a complete sentence and a kindness.

If your family is earlier in this than that, the work that matters is the training, not the logistics. Weekly classes, a conservatory production where a teenager carries real responsibility, and, when the year comes, audition coaching that starts with repertoire rather than with a calendar.

Closing reflection: the ballroom at seven in the morning

I think about that carpet a lot.

Because for the faculty behind those tables, it is a long weekend of a job they do every year. And for the kid warming up in the stairwell, it is the biggest morning of their life so far, and those two facts are both completely true at the same time, in the same building.

What I have watched, over and over, is that the students who come out of it steady are not the ones who auditioned for the most schools. They are the ones who knew why each school was on their list, who had material that actually belonged to them, and who had somebody in the hallway who was not keeping score.

That last one is you. It is the only part of this weekend that is genuinely yours to control, and it matters more than the room does.

Inspiritu,
Mr. CJ

Questions parents ask

What are unified college auditions?

Unified auditions are events where many college theatre and musical theatre programs hold auditions in one building over one weekend, so a student can audition for several schools without travelling to each campus. The word covers three different things. The National Unified Auditions are a coalition of roughly twenty-five to thirty BFA programs that audition in New York, Chicago and Los Angeles each winter. Regional unifieds, such as those in Pittsburgh, Utah and Virginia, are independent events that use the same word and the same format but have their own school lists. And United Auditions is a separate event with a confusingly similar name. No single event is attended by every program, so the school list always decides which event matters.

Do you have to go to unifieds to get into a BFA program?

No. Unifieds are a convenience, not a requirement, and every program also holds auditions on its own campus. A useful rule: count how many schools on your child's actual list will be in one building on one weekend. If it is three or more, going is usually worth the travel. If it is one or two, audition on campus instead, where slots are longer, rooms are calmer, and your child sees the place they would actually live. Note also that most musical theatre programs require a recorded prescreen in the fall, and a live audition slot is generally by invitation rather than something you can simply show up for.

How much does the college audition season cost?

Widely cited totals for a full BFA audition season run upwards of $5,000, and most of that is not audition fees. The four lines are per-school application and audition fees, which stack quickly across a dozen or more schools; prescreen production, meaning an accompanist, a room and someone competent behind a camera; travel, which is the largest line by far once flights and midwinter hotels for two people are counted; and optional coaching, which varies enormously in price. Attending a regional event within driving distance is the single biggest way families reduce the travel line.
