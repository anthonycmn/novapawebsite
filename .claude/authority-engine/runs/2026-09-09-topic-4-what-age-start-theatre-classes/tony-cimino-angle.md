**Title:** The Age Question Is a Program Design Question

**Slug:** age-question-is-a-program-design-question

**Standfirst:** Parents ask what age their child should start. They are really
asking whether you built a room their child can survive. Here is how to answer
it in your curriculum instead of in your lobby.

---

Every teacher I train gets the same lobby question, and most of them answer it
wrong. Not because the answer is hard. Because it is the wrong question and we
keep taking it at face value.

"Is she old enough?"

We say yes, because we need the enrollment. Or we say no, because the last
five-year-old melted down in week two and we are still tired. Both answers are
about the child. Neither one is about us.

Here is what I have learned in twenty years of building these rooms: when a
child fails in a class, the class was designed for a different child. That is
it. That is the whole diagnosis. A five-year-old who cannot retain blocking is
not behind. A five-year-old who cannot retain blocking is a five-year-old, and
the person who put blocking in front of her made an error in curriculum design
and then billed a family for it.

Fix systems, not children.

## Design to the floor, not to the ceiling

Every age group has a developmental floor — the thing the least-ready child in
the room can reliably do on a bad day. Design the hour to that floor. The
ceiling takes care of itself, because the ready kids will fill any space you
leave them.

At five to eight, the floor is: fifteen to thirty minutes of structured focus,
a two-step direction from an unfamiliar adult, waiting through someone else's
turn, and pretending without being asked. Build the hour out of those four
capacities and every child succeeds. Add a script and you have manufactured
failure for a third of the room.

At nine to twelve, the floor moves: fluent reading, retention across weeks,
ensemble awareness, and — the real leap — the ability to receive a note as
craft rather than as verdict. That last one is what actually unlocks technique.
Not talent. Durability.

At thirteen to seventeen, the floor drops back down, and this is the part
programs get catastrophically wrong. The instrument is changing. Self-
consciousness has arrived. A student who was fearless at ten will not want to
be looked at at fourteen. If your teen curriculum assumes the confidence of
your pre-teen curriculum, you will lose them, and you will tell yourself they
lost interest.

They did not lose interest. You stopped building for them.

## The audit

Take your class list. For each age band, write down the single hardest thing
you ask a student to do in week one. Then ask whether the least-ready child in
that band can do it.

If the answer is no, you do not have an enrollment problem or a retention
problem. You have a design problem wearing a retention problem's clothes.

Rule: **the right age to start is the age at which your room was actually built
for that child.** Which means the age question was never theirs to answer. It
was always ours.
