## Post

Every teacher has a bench. Mine is a scuffed wooden one against the back wall of the studio, and the first week of every season a child is sitting on it with a parent's arm around them, not joining in.

For years I treated the bench as a problem to solve. I crouched. I coaxed. I made the game irresistible. And I spent five minutes of a forty-five-minute class making a six-year-old the center of attention, which was the exact thing she was on the bench to avoid.

So I stopped solving the bench and built it into the room. It got a name (the best seat in the house), a job (the audience, whose work is to watch and clap), and a rule I say out loud in week one: watching is participating.

Here is the leadership lesson, and it is not about six-year-olds.

The person who is not joining in is giving you information about the room, not about themselves. The teacher silent in the faculty meeting. The volunteer standing by the wall. The stage manager who confirms the cue three times. Each one is telling you what the room costs and whether they can afford it yet.

Coaxing raises the price. Structure lowers it. Name the watching. Give a wordless job. Run the same order every time.

Build the bench into the room. Then watch for the rain.

Inspiritu,
Mr. CJ
