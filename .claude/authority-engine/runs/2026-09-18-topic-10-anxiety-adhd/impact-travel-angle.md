Every teacher who has run a student trip to New York knows the two kids I mean. The one who cannot stop moving on a four-hour bus, and the one who has read the itinerary eleven times and still asks what happens after lunch.

I've taken both to Broadway more times than I can count, and I wrote about why the same rehearsal room works for both of them in [a piece for parents](https://novapa.org/blog/drama-for-children-with-anxiety-or-adhd-tony-cimino-johnson.html). The trip version is the same principle with a bus attached: build the accommodation into the plan, not into the chaperone.

## Five things the plan does for you

1. **Print the itinerary, hour by hour, and hand it to every student.** Not a link. Paper. The anxious kid reads it and puts the scanner down; the ADHD kid reads it and knows what he is running toward. You stop answering "what's next" forty times.
2. **Announce every transition ten minutes out.** "Ten minutes, then we walk to the theatre." Say it to the whole group at every stop. Nobody has to be singled out.
3. **Give the mover a job with motion in it.** Headcount at every door. Bag check on the bus. The kid who cannot sit is the kid who can count thirty-one heads in a lobby faster than any adult.
4. **Build a decompression slot into every day.** Thirty minutes back at the hotel between the last activity and dinner. It costs nothing and it is the difference between a meltdown at 9 p.m. and a quiet one that never happens.
5. **Decide in advance what happens when someone gets it wrong.** Late to the lobby, lost a MetroCard, wandered. The response is set before the trip: a calm redo, not a public scene. Anxious kids are watching how you handle the first mistake to decide whether they can afford to make one.

Rule: if you find a chaperone running a special system for one student, ask whether the plan could do it for everyone. On a trip, it almost always can, and it is cheaper than a chaperone's patience at day three.

The ethics valve: none of this replaces a parent's information or a student's own plan with their doctor. Ask families before the trip what works for their child, in writing, and read it. Then build the plan so that most of it is already handled before the bus leaves the parking lot.

Bring both of those kids. They are, in my experience, the ones who remember the trip longest.
