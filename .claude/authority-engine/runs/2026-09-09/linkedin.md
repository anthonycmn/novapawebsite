**Post (≈250 words, first person, leadership/craft register):**

Fifteen seconds before a young performer sings a note, most of my casting decision is already running.

How they entered. Whether the sheet music was in a binder or on a phone. Whether they gave the accompanist a tempo or a shrug. Whether they looked at me once, like a person.

None of that was on my rubric for years. All of it was in my notes.

That is not a character flaw in directors. It is an unexamined system. When the criteria that actually drive a decision live in a director's instincts instead of on a printed page, students cannot study for them. Families invent folklore to fill the gap. And the students with a coach or a theatre-literate parent arrive with an advantage that has nothing to do with talent.

That is not a meritocracy. That is an access problem wearing a rubric's clothes.

So we started publishing the whole assessment. Our audition notices now name what gets scored, including the walk-in and the thank-you. We teach the mechanics as curriculum rather than insider knowledge: how to cut sixteen bars, how to mark an accompanist's copy, how to transpose into your own key and why that is not cheating. A student who does not know those things is not less talented. They are less briefed.

Briefing is free.

I wrote the parent-facing version this week — what directors are actually listening for, and the songs that work by age. Link in the comments.

If you run an audition process of any kind, hiring included: publish your criteria. All of them. The people who benefit most are the ones who never had anyone to explain the first fifteen seconds.
