Every October, after the parent meeting ends and the room clears, one parent waits. The question is always the same, and it is never about the show. "She loves it. But does theatre actually look good on college applications?"

For the first several years of my career I answered it with a speech. I had the speech down. Collaboration, deadlines, public accountability, the letter I could write that no AP teacher could. It was a good speech. And every spring, a junior still sat across from me with a schedule form and told me they were dropping theatre for another AP because it would look better.

So I stopped explaining.

The speech was asking families to trust me over the transcript. The transcript was always going to win, because the transcript is the thing colleges read. If I wanted theatre to count, it had to count on the page, in a language admissions offices already spoke.

That is how Virginia's first Dual Enrollment Theatre Program came to exist at Rock Ridge High School. In partnership with Richard Bland College of William & Mary, our theatre courses began carrying college credit. A student in my acting class was, on paper, a college student earning transferable hours, and when a $32,000 grant from the Jack Kent Cooke Foundation arrived, the program grew into stagecraft as well, so the kid who welded and the kid who ran the light board were earning the same credit as the kid who sang. Some years between fifteen and twenty percent of the school was enrolled in a theatre course.

Here is what I learned building it, and it applies to any program leader, in any field.

**Rule: when the people you serve keep being forced to choose between what they love and what is rewarded, stop persuading and start building.** The junior with the schedule form was not wrong. They were responding rationally to the incentives in front of them. My job was not to out-argue the incentives. It was to change them.

**The system has to speak the institution's language.** "Theatre teaches collaboration" is true and invisible. "THR 101, 3 credits, Richard Bland College" is visible. Structural Legitimacy, the third of the Five Pillars, is exactly this: the program exists in the paperwork, not only in the room.

**Build for the kids who are leaving.** The theatre majors were going to stay regardless. The program was built for the future nurse and the future engineer, so that the thing they loved could travel with them. Those students are the ones who came back years later to say the credit mattered and the room mattered more.

The parent in the hallway still asks the question. I still answer it, and the parent-facing version, including how to describe four years of theatre on the Common App so a reader can give credit for it, is on the NOVAPA blog. But the answer got shorter the year the system existed, because I no longer had to ask anyone to take my word for it.

Passion gets students in the room. Systems keep them there.

Inspiritu,
Mr. CJ
