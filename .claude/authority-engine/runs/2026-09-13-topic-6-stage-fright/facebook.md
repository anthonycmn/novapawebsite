## Post

The seahorse would not go on.

Second grader, purple felt, both hands wrapped around a light boom in the
stage left wing, eyes on the floor. Her mom had told me at pickup that week:
"She's been so nervous, we've been practicing every night, I keep telling her
she's going to be great."

I did not say anything at pickup because there was a line behind her. I am
saying it now, because the fall season is starting and a lot of families are
about to do exactly this, out of nothing but love.

Practicing every night the week of the show tells a seven-year-old that the
grown-ups are worried. "You're going to be great" makes great the standard, so
one skipped word becomes a failure. And "don't be nervous" gives a kid two
problems instead of one.

So I wrote down what actually works, from the wings instead of a pamphlet. The
three different things parents mean when they say stage fright, and why they
need different answers. What we actually do backstage when a child locks up
(nobody publishes this, so I did). What to say at the stage door and what to
say in the car. And, carefully, how to tell when it is more than stage fright
and the right call is your pediatrician, not your director.

The seahorse went on. She went on holding the crab's hand, half a beat late,
and nobody in the fourth row could tell you which one of them was late.

Mr. CJ
