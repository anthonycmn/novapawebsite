What Are Unified Auditions? A Plain-English Guide for Families

By Tony Cimino-Johnson · Founder & CEO, Northern Virginia Performing Arts

Seven in the morning in a conference center ballroom, and the carpet is that pattern they make specifically for conference centers, the one designed to hide everything. Tables going up along three walls, one per school, a paper sign taped to the front of each. Two coffee urns that will be empty by nine. And somewhere down the hall a seventeen year old is warming up in a stairwell, because the stairwell has better acoustics than any room in the building. It always does. Every building, forever.

I've set that room up. My students call me Mr. CJ, and along with running NOVAPA I run DC Unifieds, a regional college audition weekend in Leesburg, which means I've spent a lot of hours on the other side of those tables. Assigning rooms. Chasing down a school's rep who has wandered off to find breakfast. Watching a parent stand in the doorway with a garment bag over one arm, trying to work out where to put herself.

And every year, from that doorway, I get some version of the same question.

"So we have to go to Chicago in February?"

No. Probably not. And the reason you think you do is that the word is a mess, and nobody in my field has ever bothered to clean it up.

Three different things, one word

This is not your fault. "Unifieds" is doing three jobs at once.

The National Unified Auditions are the original. A loose coalition of somewhere around twenty-five to thirty BFA theatre and musical theatre programs that agree to audition in the same three cities every winter: New York, Chicago, and Los Angeles. Chicago is the big one and runs the most days. LA is the smallest. This is what people mean when they say Unifieds with a capital U, and the member list is public at unifiedauditions.com.

Regional unifieds are independent events that use the same word because they do the same thing: a lot of schools, one building, one weekend. Pittsburgh has one. Utah has one. Western New York has one. Mine is one. We're not run by the coalition, we don't share a school list, and we each work a bit differently.

"United Auditions" is a fourth thing with a name so close to the others that families mix it up constantly, and honestly, who could blame them.

So when a school's website says "we will be at Unifieds," that sentence isn't finished. Which ones? Every program publishes its own list, and there is no single event that all of them attend. Let me be clear, because this is the part that costs families money: the one official audition you absolutely must get to does not exist.

What actually happens in the room

Here's the part almost nobody writes down, mostly because almost nobody writing about this has worked one.

Each school gets a space. Sometimes a hotel suite with the bed taken out, sometimes a classroom, sometimes a corner of a ballroom behind pipe and drape. One or two faculty sit at a table. Your kid comes in, hands over a headshot and résumé, says their name and what they're singing, and does sixteen or thirty-two bars and a monologue. Five minutes, give or take.

Then there's the dance call, which is separate and much bigger, forty kids learning a combination in a room with mirrors while schools watch from the side. And there are information sessions, which families skip because they feel optional and which are, from where I sit, one of the most useful hours of the whole weekend.

What the faculty are doing in those five minutes is less dramatic than you're afraid it is. They're not weighing your child's worth. They're making a first sort: is this person trainable, are they right for what we do here, did they pick material that actually suits them. Somebody who liked your kid writes a note and follows up. Somebody who didn't has a freshman class of sixteen and has seen four hundred people since Thursday.

Which is the thing I most want parents to take out of this. A no from a program that trains heavy classical voice, when your kid belts contemporary pop, isn't a verdict on your kid. It's two lists that didn't overlap. I know that's easier to read than to feel at nine o'clock that night in a hotel room, but it's true, and it stays true.

Prescreens come first, and they catch everyone out

Most musical theatre programs now want a prescreen: recorded songs, a monologue, sometimes a dance reel, submitted through a platform (usually Acceptd) in the fall, months before anybody stands in a ballroom. Schools watch those and decide who gets a live slot at all.

Which quietly reorders your whole year. The panic that hits families in January is aimed at the wrong month. The real deadline was October. If you've got a junior right now, build backward from prescreen dates, not from audition dates.

It also means a live audition is usually something you're invited to rather than something you show up for. Walk-in slots exist at some programs. They're limited, and the lines for them are the least fun thing about any audition weekend.

So do you have to go?

No. Nobody has to. Every program in the country also auditions on its own campus, and a campus visit will show your child things a ballroom never can, like what the building smells like at four in the afternoon and whether the students there seem happy.

But I want to push back on the way most families decide this, because I think the common advice is wrong.

What people are usually told is to count how many schools from their list will be in the building, and go only if the number is high enough. I understand the arithmetic. I also think it misses the entire point, and I say that as someone who has watched what actually happens in these rooms.

Here's what happens. A family comes for three schools they've researched to death. Somewhere around Saturday afternoon, with a gap in the schedule and nothing else to do, their kid walks into a room for a program nobody in the family had heard of six hours earlier. Maybe a rep grabbed them in a hallway. Maybe the schedule just worked out that way. And that's the one. That's the conversation they're still talking about in the car on the way home, and eight months later that's where they're going.

I have seen it more times than I can count, and it is the single best argument for being in the building. A list built at a kitchen table in September is made of websites and rankings and whatever a cousin said. The list you have after a weekend of actually meeting people is made of something better. You cannot discover a program you've never heard of by driving to a campus you already chose.

So count your schools, sure. Then add the ones you haven't met yet, because they're the reason to go.

What it costs, honestly

Nobody itemizes this, so here it is. The number that gets passed around for a full BFA audition season is upwards of $5,000, and it's real, and most of it isn't audition fees.

  • Application and audition fees. Per school, and they stack up fast once the list gets past a dozen.
  • Prescreen production. An accompanist, a room, somebody competent behind a camera. Families either spend money here or spend a weekend learning to do it themselves, and both are legitimate.
  • Travel. The big one, by a distance. Flights and a midwinter hotel in New York or Chicago, for two people, for three or four nights.
  • Coaching. Optional, genuinely useful, and priced all over the map. I do this work, and I'll still tell you it's the first line to cut if something has to go.

This is the honest case for a regional event, and I'll state my interest plainly since I run one: driving ninety minutes and sleeping in your own bed is a completely different financial event than flying to Chicago. If that's what stands between your kid and a weekend in front of thirty schools, it matters. Registration for DC Unifieds is here, and if you're anywhere on the East Coast, it's worth a look at which schools are coming.

What I'd tell you if you were standing in my doorway

Build the school list first, then the calendar. The list is the strategy. Unifieds are logistics.

Go to the information sessions. Your kid is exhausted and wants to go lie down. Go anyway.

Let your child choose their own material, with help. Faculty can tell inside eight bars when a song was picked by an adult who wanted to impress them, and it reads as exactly what it is.

Feed everybody. I have watched more auditions come apart from a skipped lunch than from a wrong song, and it isn't close.

And work out in advance what you're going to say in the car afterward. "How did it go?" is a much harder question than it sounds when your child genuinely doesn't know and can't know. "I'm proud of you, what do you want for dinner" is a whole sentence and a kindness.

If you're earlier in this than that, none of the above is the work. The work is the training: weekly classes, a conservatory production where a teenager carries something real, and when the year comes, audition coaching that starts with repertoire instead of a calendar.

Closing reflection: the ballroom at seven in the morning

I think about that carpet more than a person should.

Because for the faculty behind those tables it's a long weekend of a job they do every year, and for the kid in the stairwell it's the biggest morning of their life so far, and both of those things are entirely true, at the same time, in the same building.

The students I've watched come out of it steady were never the ones who auditioned for the most schools. They were the ones who knew why each school was on the list, who had material that actually belonged to them, and who had somebody out in the hallway who wasn't keeping score.

That last one is you. It's the only part of the weekend that's genuinely yours, and it matters more than the room does.

Questions parents ask

What are unified college auditions?

Unified auditions are events where many college theatre and musical theatre programs hold auditions in one building over one weekend, so a student can audition for several schools without travelling to each campus. The word covers three different things. The National Unified Auditions are a coalition of roughly twenty-five to thirty BFA programs that audition in New York, Chicago and Los Angeles each winter. Regional unifieds, such as those in Pittsburgh, Utah and Virginia, are independent events that use the same word and the same format but have their own school lists. And United Auditions is a separate event with a confusingly similar name. No single event is attended by every program, so the school list always decides which event matters.

Do you have to go to unifieds, and is it worth it?

No, and every program also auditions on its own campus, so nothing about a unified event is compulsory. But the common advice — count how many schools from your list will be in the building and go only if that number is high enough — misses what these weekends are actually good for. The programs your child has not heard of yet are the best reason to be there. Families routinely arrive for three schools they have researched to death and leave talking about a fourth they walked into on the Saturday afternoon, and you cannot discover a program you have never heard of by driving to a campus you already chose. Note also that most musical theatre programs require a recorded prescreen in the fall, and a live audition slot is generally by invitation rather than something you can simply show up for.

How much does the college audition season cost?

Widely cited totals for a full BFA audition season run upwards of $5,000, and most of that is not audition fees. The four lines are per-school application and audition fees, which stack quickly across a dozen or more schools; prescreen production, meaning an accompanist, a room and someone competent behind a camera; travel, which is the largest line by far once flights and midwinter hotels for two people are counted; and optional coaching, which varies enormously in price. Attending a regional event within driving distance is the single biggest way families cut the travel line, which is why regional unifieds such as DC Unifieds in Leesburg, Virginia have grown on the East Coast.
