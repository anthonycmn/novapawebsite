## Get Low: What a Frozen Seven-Year-Old Taught Me About Managing Adults

There is a protocol I train every stage manager and teaching artist on for a
child who locks up in the wing. I wrote it down years ago because I kept
watching well-meaning adults do the opposite, and I kept watching it fail.

Get low, so your face is below theirs. Do not ask what is wrong. Give the body
one job. Give them a job that is about somebody else. Talk to the character,
not the child. Send them on with the group, never alone. And if they still
cannot, they do not go, and the show survives.

Here is the thing I did not expect when I wrote it. Every one of those steps is
about adults.

## The tall adult leaning in

Watch a manager with an employee who has frozen in a meeting. The manager
leans forward. Towers, without meaning to. Asks, kindly, "what's going on with
you?" And the employee, who is at threshold and does not know what is going
on, now has a question to fail on top of everything else.

Get low. Not literally, in a conference room, but the posture matters. Make
yourself smaller than the problem. Then skip the question. A person at
threshold cannot answer it and should not have to.

## Muscles and breath, then a task

What a frozen nervous system needs is something physical with an end. Press
your feet into the floor. Blow out a candle, slow. Adults will not do this if
you name it, so you build it in: the walk to the coffee machine, the thing to
carry to the other room, the five-minute break that is actually a body reset
with a polite name.

Then give them a responsibility for someone else. "Can you make sure the crab
gets on with you?" is the most reliable exit from self-watching I have ever
found, and it works on a forty-year-old exactly the way it works on a
seven-year-old. A person who is responsible for another person stops being the
subject of their own attention.

## Never alone, and permission to not go on

The entrance that goes worst is always the solo. The clump of sea creatures
almost always makes it. Nervous systems regulate off each other; that is not a
metaphor. Put the struggling person next to someone.

And when none of it works, they do not go on. Not this time. A person who is
forced across a freeze learns that this is a place where their body gets
overruled, and that lesson takes years to undo. The show survives. The meeting
survives. Let them not go on.

Fear is loudest when a person is alone and watching themselves. It gets quiet
when they are busy and part of something. That is the whole design brief. The
parent-facing version, with the full backstage protocol, is on the NOVAPA blog.
