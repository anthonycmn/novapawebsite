Every teacher has a bench. Mine is a real one, a scuffed wooden thing against the back wall of the studio, and the first week of every season somebody's child is sitting on it with a parent's arm around them, not joining in.

For the first few years of my career I treated the bench as a problem to solve. I would crouch, I would coax, I would make the game so irresistible that surely, surely, the child would slide off and join. Sometimes it worked. More often the child slid further into the parent and I had spent five minutes of a forty-five-minute class making a six-year-old the center of attention, which was the exact thing she was on the bench to avoid.

So I stopped solving the bench.

Instead I built it into the room. The bench got a name (the best seat in the house), a job (the audience, whose work is to watch and clap), and a rule I say out loud in week one, to the whole circle: watching is participating. Nobody performs alone in the first month. Every child gets a job with no words in it. The class runs in the same order every week, warm-up to bow, so a slow-to-warm kid can stop scanning and start playing.

The bench stopped being where the shy kids were stuck and became where the season started for about a third of them.

Here is the leadership principle, and it has nothing to do with six-year-olds.

**The person who is not joining in is giving you information about the room, not about themselves.** A teacher who never speaks in the faculty meeting, a stage manager who will not call the cue until she has confirmed it three times, a parent volunteer who shows up and stands by the wall: every one of them is telling you what the room costs and whether they can afford it yet. Coaxing raises the price. Structure lowers it.

Three moves that lower the price of any room:

1. **Name the watching.** Say, out loud, that observing is a legitimate first job. People who are given permission to watch join faster than people who are told to join.
2. **Give a wordless job.** Hold the prop. Run the timer. Take the notes. A role with no spotlight in it gets a person inside the circle without asking them to be brave first. Once they are inside, the rest is time.
3. **Run the same order every time.** Predictability is not boring. It is the thing that lets a nervous system stop guarding and start contributing. Every high-functioning rehearsal room, faculty, and booster club I have ever run had a fixed shape that people could see coming.

I trained in drama therapy at the graduate level because I kept meeting the child on the bench and wanted better tools than charm. What I learned is that distance is a gift: a person can be brave inside a role, a job, or a routine long before they can be brave as themselves, and the courage transfers. It does not go back when the mask comes off.

The parent-facing version of this, including what to say in the car and when a drama class is not the right first step, is on the NOVAPA blog: https://novapa.org/blog/is-my-child-too-shy-for-drama-class-tony-cimino-johnson.html

Build the bench into the room. Then watch for the rain.
