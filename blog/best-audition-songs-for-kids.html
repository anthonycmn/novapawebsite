The Best Audition Songs for Kids, From the Director's Side of the Table

By Tony Cimino-Johnson · Founder & CEO, Northern Virginia Performing Arts

A nine-year-old walked to the middle of the floor at one of our auditions, handed the accompanist a phone with a lyric video on it, planted her feet, and sang thirty-two bars of a song written for a grown woman in a key nobody could have sung. She got through eleven of them.

Then she stopped, looked at me, and said: "Can I just sing the one I sing in the car?"

She sang it. It was ordinary, it was in her range, and she meant every word of it. She was cast that night.

The song is not the audition. The song is the window.

I have sat behind that table for more than twenty years, and I can tell you what almost every list of the best audition songs for kids gets wrong: it sorts songs by how impressive they sound. Directors do not sort them that way. We are listening for range, for pitch, for whether a child can hold a thought while music happens around them, and for one thing that no song list can give you, which is whether we would enjoy spending eleven weeks of rehearsal with this human being.

So here is the honest version. What we listen for, what we quietly dread, what actually works at each age, and how to cut it.

What Directors Are Actually Listening For in a Kid's Audition Song

Let me be clear: in the first fifteen seconds, we are answering four questions, and none of them are "is this a good song."

  1. Where does this voice live? Comfortable low notes, comfortable high notes, and where it starts to strain. That is casting information. A song that sits in a child's easy middle tells me more than a song that shows off two big notes and hides everything else.
  2. Can they hold a pitch and a tempo with accompaniment? Singing in the car is a solo sport. Singing with a piano is a duet, and it is a genuinely different skill.
  3. Do they mean anything? A child who understands that "I Whistle a Happy Tune" is about being scared and doing it anyway is already acting. That is the whole job.
  4. What happens when something goes wrong? The accompanist takes it faster than expected. A word disappears. I watch what the child does in that half second, because that half second is what tech week is made of.

Notice what is not on that list. Nobody is scoring difficulty. A hard song sung at seventy percent reads as a warning. A simple song sung at a hundred percent reads as a professional.

The Overdone Audition Songs Directors Quietly Dread

Every director has a list. Mine, for children, is short and predictable: "Tomorrow" and "Maybe" from Annie. "Castle on a Cloud" from Les Misérables. "Let It Go" and "Do You Want to Build a Snowman?" from Frozen. "Part of Your World." "Naughty" from Matilda. "Popular" and "Defying Gravity" from Wicked. "Santa Fe" from Newsies, which has quietly become the boys' version of "Defying Gravity."

Now the part the listicles will not tell you.

An overdone song sung beautifully beats an obscure song sung badly, every single time.

At a youth or community audition, "overdone" is not a disqualifier. It is a handicap, and a small one. The real cost is comparison: when a child sings "Let It Go," an eleven-time Broadway veteran belting it in a movie theatre comes into the room too, and your child now has company they did not invite. A less familiar song of the same emotional type removes that ghost. The director hears your child, alone, for the first time. That is worth something real.

So the rule is not "avoid famous songs." The rule is this: if your child sings an overdone song, they had better own it completely. If they cannot, trade sideways to something similar and less traveled. Same feeling, same range, no ghost.

Best Audition Songs for Kids Ages 6 to 9

At this age the priorities are memorization, comfort, and story. Short songs. Real sentences. Nothing that requires a belt.

  • "I Whistle a Happy Tune" | The King and I. Small range, and the child gets to play courage instead of just singing pretty.
  • "Happiness" | You're a Good Man, Charlie Brown. Gentle, list-shaped, endearing, almost impossible to oversing.
  • "It's Possible" | Seussical. Playful and rhythmic, and it lets a kid be delighted, which reads beautifully.
  • "Gary, Indiana" | The Music Man. The best character song in the repertoire for a young boy who can talk-sing with charm.
  • "Where Is Love?" | Oliver!. Yes, it is known. It is also fifty percent less common at auditions than "Castle on a Cloud," and it is honest.
  • "Never Never Land" | Peter Pan. Lyrical, legit, and a lovely test of sustained tone.

Best Audition Songs for Kids Ages 10 to 13

Now we can see personality, comic timing, and the beginning of real vocal ability. These are the songs I hear go well in our Teen Conservatory auditions and in school musical rooms across Loudoun and Fairfax.

  • "Alone in the Universe" | Seussical. Warm, sincere, sits in a great tween range, and it is not yet worn out.
  • "The Girl I Mean to Be" | The Secret Garden. Beautiful legit writing for a young soprano who does not want to belt.
  • "I'm Not That Smart" | The 25th Annual Putnam County Spelling Bee. Comedy, character, and a kid actually thinking on stage.
  • "Pulled" | The Addams Family. Perfect for a tween with humor and a little edge, and much rarer than "Naughty."
  • "Proud of Your Boy" | Aladdin. It has become popular for a reason: a young man gets to be vulnerable rather than loud.
  • "Watch What Happens" | Newsies. Fast, verbal, and a genuine test of diction, for the kid who has it.
  • "When I Grow Up" | Matilda. Simple line, big heart, and it does not carry the ghost that "Naughty" does.

Two hard rules with any of these. First, do not sing from the show being cast unless the audition notice asks for it, because the director will hear the role you want instead of the actor you are. Second, sing it in your child's key, not the original key. Transposition is not cheating. It is the entire point of bringing sheet music.

How to Pick an Audition Song for Your Child: Four Questions

Skip the lists for a moment, including mine, and run the candidate song through this:

  1. Can they sing it from memory, cold, in the car, tomorrow? If not, it is the wrong song. Memory is the floor, not the ceiling.
  2. Is the highest note comfortable on a bad day? Audition mornings are bad days. Colds happen. Nerves take the top of a range away.
  3. Can they tell you what the song is about in one sentence, in their own words? If they cannot, they will sing sounds instead of meaning, and we hear it instantly.
  4. Does it match the world of the show? Golden-age show, legit song. Contemporary pop-rock show, contemporary song. Sondheim, please no pop ballad.

If all four are yes, that is the song. Do not keep shopping. The most common mistake I see is a family switching songs eight days before the audition because someone in a Facebook group said the first one was overdone. A new song at eighty percent memorized is a worse bet than an overdone song at a hundred percent.

How to Cut Sixteen Bars Without Wrecking the Song

Most youth auditions ask for 16 or 32 bars, which is roughly thirty to sixty seconds. Here is the mechanic, and it is not complicated:

  • Cut to the best singing, not the beginning. The opening verse is often the least interesting part. Start where the song gets somewhere.
  • End on a resolved phrase. A cut that stops mid-sentence makes a child look unprepared for a decision an adult made.
  • Show two notes, not one. Include a comfortable low and a comfortable high. Range is casting data.
  • Mark it heavily. Two thick vertical lines where the accompanist starts, two where they stop, in the correct key, printed, in a binder or taped, single-sided. Not a phone. Never a phone.
  • Rehearse the eight seconds before and after. Walking in, saying a name and a title clearly, giving the tempo, and saying thank you at the end. Those eight seconds are half of what we remember.

If a family wants outside help with exactly this, one session is usually enough at this age. A single private coaching hour runs about $75 to $150 across the DC metro area, and to keep my own numbers honest: a 50-minute private lesson at our studio in Leesburg is $120. I share the figure as a benchmark, not a pitch. Most children do not need a course of lessons to audition well. They need one clear song, cut properly, learned cold.

What Parents Get Wrong, Gently

Three things, and I say them with enormous affection, because I have watched hundreds of loving parents do all three.

We push difficulty when we should push comfort. We chase the song that will "show them what she can do," when the director is asking a much smaller question. And we sit in the car afterward and ask "how did it go," which teaches a child that the audition was a test they might have failed.

Ask a different question. Ask what the room was like. Ask who else was there. Ask whether they had fun. Auditioning is a skill built by repetition, not by outcome, and a child who enjoys auditioning will out-earn a talented child who dreads it within two seasons. That is why we build audition practice into our weekly musical theatre classes rather than saving it for the day it counts, and why the same logic scales all the way up to college auditions years later.

Closing Reflection: The One She Sings in the Car

That nine-year-old is in high school now. She still auditions. She is very good at it, and I would like to tell you it is because we found her the perfect song, but that is not what happened.

What happened is that she learned, at nine, that the room wanted her and not a performance of somebody else.

They will not remember which song they sang for their first audition.

They will remember whether they were allowed to sound like themselves.

They will remember whether the grown-ups in the room were on their side.

Pick the song that lets your child be recognizable. Everything else is repertoire.

Questions parents ask

What are the best audition songs for kids?

The one that sits in their comfortable middle range, is short enough to know cold, and is about something they can actually mean. Ages 6–9: "I Whistle a Happy Tune," "Happiness," "It's Possible," "Gary, Indiana," "Where Is Love?" Ages 10–13: "Alone in the Universe," "The Girl I Mean to Be," "I'm Not That Smart," "Pulled," "Proud of Your Boy." Skip pop songs unless the notice asks for one, and never sing from the show being cast unless the director requests it.

Should my child avoid overdone songs like "Tomorrow" or "Let It Go"?

Not automatically. An overdone song sung well beats an obscure song sung badly every time. But famous songs bring a famous performance into the room with them, and your child is then compared to it. If they own the song completely, sing it. If not, trade sideways: same feeling, same range, less traveled.

How long should a child's audition song be?

Follow the notice — usually 16 or 32 bars, about 30 to 60 seconds. Cut to the best singing rather than the beginning, end on a resolved phrase, and mark the start and stop with two heavy vertical lines on printed sheet music in your child's key. Rehearse the walk-in and the thank-you too; those eight seconds are half of what a director remembers.
