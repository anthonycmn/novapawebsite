Two emails, same morning, the week rehearsals start.

"He has ADHD. I'm worried he'll be a disruption."
"She has anxiety. Please tell me if I'm pushing her."

For years I answered those emails the way a tired teacher does: reassurance, a policy, a promise to keep an eye on him.

Keeping an eye on him was the problem. So I stopped answering the emails and started answering the room.

Here is what changed. Instead of running a separate accommodation for every child who needed one (fourteen of them by tech week, none run well), I built the accommodations into the structure of the rehearsal itself, so they ran whether I was watching or not:

Movement before listening, every day, including the day we're behind.
Every transition announced to the whole room.
The plan posted in the same corner, every rehearsal.
A job for every body, with motion in it.
A ritual for mistakes: laughter, "again," a redo. Never silence.

The boy from the first email became my assistant stage manager for entrances in week two. By week five he had every actor's blocking memorized. His mother feared a disruption. She got infrastructure.

The girl from the second email learned the entire score in her bedroom before the first rehearsal so nothing could surprise her. By the show she was the funniest thing in it.

Neither child got a special system. They got a room with a system in it.

The lesson for anyone who leads a room full of people: if you find yourself running a separate accommodation for one person, ask whether the room could do it for everyone. It usually can. It usually costs less. And the people who needed it most will never have to be singled out to receive it.

Build the room once. Let it do the work.
