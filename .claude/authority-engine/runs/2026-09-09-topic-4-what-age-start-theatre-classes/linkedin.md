## Post

A parent asked me last year whether her son was too young to start.

I said yes, he's ready. Eight months later he was center stage in a red felt
crown, walking an entire show on his knees as Lord Farquaad, holding two hundred
people before he said a word.

I have been answering that question for twenty years, and I have finally
accepted that it is the wrong question — and that we are the ones who made it
the wrong question.

When a child fails in one of our classes, the class was designed for a different
child. That is the whole diagnosis. A five-year-old who cannot retain blocking
is not behind. She is five. The adult who put blocking in front of her made a
curriculum error and then billed a family for it.

So here is the design principle I now train every teacher on: build the hour to
the developmental floor, not the ceiling. The floor is what the least-ready
child in the room can do on a bad day. At five to eight that is fifteen minutes
of focus, a two-step direction from an unfamiliar adult, waiting a turn, and
pretending unprompted. Build from those four and every child succeeds. Add a
script and you have manufactured failure for a third of the room.

The ceiling takes care of itself. Ready kids fill any space you leave them.

If your program has a retention problem in a particular age band, look at week
one and ask what the hardest thing you demanded was. Most retention problems are
design problems wearing a retention problem's clothes.

Fix systems, not children.

Inspiritu,
Mr. CJ
