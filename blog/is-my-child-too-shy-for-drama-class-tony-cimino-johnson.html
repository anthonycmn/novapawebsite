Is My Child Too Shy for Drama Class? What Twenty Years of First Days Taught Me

By Tony Cimino-Johnson · Founder & CEO, Northern Virginia Performing Arts

Monday, 6:00 p.m., the first week of a new season. The Acting class for ages five to eight is on the floor in a circle, and a girl I will call Nora is not in it. She's in the doorway, one hand wrapped around her mother's leg, the other holding the door frame like the building might tip. Her mother catches my eye over her head and mouths the sentence I've heard from a thousand doorways in twenty years:

"I'm so sorry. She's just really shy."

Twenty years in, my students call me Mr. CJ, and I am here to tell you that the sentence in that doorway is never a problem for me, and it's never a problem for the class. The only person it's a problem for is the child hearing it.

So let's answer the real question, the one you typed into a search bar at eleven at night: is your child too shy for drama class?

Will drama classes help a shy child? The honest answer

Yes. And the reason is more specific than "it builds confidence," which is what every studio website on earth will tell you.

A shy child is almost never afraid of pretending. Watch them at home: they run entire kingdoms under the dining room table, voice every stuffed animal, narrate the dog's inner life. What a shy child is afraid of is being watched as themselves. The eyes on them. The moment where the room goes quiet and waits.

Drama solves that problem in a way no other activity can, because drama hands the child three things to stand behind: a character, a group, and a game. Nobody in a good first-week class is asked to be Nora in front of twelve strangers. She's asked to be a tree, in a forest of trees, in a game where the trees all sway on the count of three. That is not a smaller version of performing. It is the whole art form working exactly as designed.

Drama therapists have a word for this. They call it distance. A child can be brave as the wolf long before she can be brave as herself, and the courage she borrows from the wolf does not go back when she takes the mask off. It stays. I've watched it stay for twenty years.

Put simply: your child is not too shy for drama class. Drama class is the thing that was built for shy children. The question is whether the room you're walking into was built right.

Shy, anxious, or quiet: three kids who look the same in the doorway

Before you enroll anybody, it helps to know which child you have, because the internet lumps all three together and they need different things.

The shy child is slow to warm. New rooms cost them something, and they pay it by watching first. Given time and a predictable structure, they warm up, and once they've warmed up they are often the most committed kid in the room, because they chose it carefully. This child belongs in drama class, and the only thing the class has to do is not rush them.

The quiet child is not shy at all. They just don't need to be loud. They will happily play the narrator, the stage manager, the kid who remembers everyone's blocking. Quiet is a temperament, not a deficit, and a good teacher will give this child a job with a voice in it and then get out of their way. I have had quiet kids run a light board with five hundred and twenty-six cues in it who never once volunteered a line in class. Nobody on that crew doubted who was in charge.

The anxious child is different, and this is the part the franchise blogs skip. Anxiety is not slow to warm; it is avoidance with a body attached. Stomachaches on the drive. Tears that don't stop when the game starts. A refusal that holds for weeks, not minutes. If that's your child, drama class can still be part of the answer, but it is not the first step, and I'll say more about that below. I trained in drama therapy at the graduate level precisely because I kept meeting this child and wanted to help more carefully.

Most children who arrive in a doorway are the first kind. Nora was the first kind. Here is what happened to her.

What a good drama class does for a shy child in the first month

Week one, Nora sat on the bench with her mother. I said hello to her once, at her eye level, told her the bench was the best seat in the house, and gave her a job: she was the audience, and the audience's job is to watch and clap at the end. Then I left her alone. She watched the entire class. She clapped at the end.

Week two, same bench. Halfway through, during a game where the whole circle turns into a storm, I saw her hands moving in her lap. Rain. She was playing from the bench.

Week three, I put out a chair inside the circle instead of on the bench and said nothing about it. She sat in it. When the forest game came around, she was a tree. Trees don't have lines. Trees sway.

Week six, Nora was the wolf. The wolf has one line, and she said it to the whole room, and she said it loud, and afterward she looked at the door where her mother was standing with her hand over her mouth.

She was not too shy for drama class. She was too shy to be watched alone, and nobody ever asked her to be.

That is the system, and I want to name its parts, because you should expect them from any class you pay for:

  • Watching counts as participating. The bench is not a punishment or a holding pen. It is week one for a lot of kids, and a teacher who understands shy children says so out loud.
  • Nobody performs alone in the first month. Group games, whole-circle characters, choral lines. The spotlight, if it comes, comes in week five or six, by invitation, and can be declined.
  • Every child gets a job with no words in it. Prop holder. Audience. Sound effects. Tree. A job gives a shy child a reason to be in the room that isn't "be brave."
  • The class runs the same way every week. Warm-up, game, story, share, bow. Predictability is what lets a slow-to-warm child stop scanning the room and start playing.
  • The teacher never coaxes in front of the group. "Come on, Nora, everybody's waiting" is the fastest way to lose a shy child for the season. A good teacher offers once, privately, and moves on.

Rule: give it three classes before you judge, and six to eight weeks before you look for the change at home. The change at home is the real one. It shows up as a kid who orders her own food at the restaurant, or raises a hand in second grade, or tells you about the wolf.

Drama classes for shy kids: what to look for, and what to walk away from

Here in Loudoun County there are a lot of options, which is exactly why you need a way to tell them apart. Price won't do it: a weekly children's drama class in Leesburg, Ashburn, or Fairfax runs roughly $80 to $120 a month, and ours at Northern Virginia Performing Arts is $90. Sit in on one class, or ask these questions, and the right room will be obvious.

Reflective considerations, from the doorway:

  • Does the class start the same way every week, with a routine a child could predict by week two?
  • Is there a way to participate without speaking in the first few weeks, and does the teacher name it as real participation?
  • Does the teacher greet a hesitant child at eye level, once, and then let them be?
  • Is there a recital or showcase, and if so, is it a group piece a child can hide inside, or a lineup of solos?
  • Can your child try a class before you commit? (At NOVAPA the first class is free, and that is on purpose: the shy child's first class is the one where nothing should be at stake, including money.)
  • How does the teacher talk about your child in front of your child?

If two or more of those answers make you wince, keep looking. It isn't your child. It's the room.

Walk away from any class where the pitch is "we'll get them out of their shell." The shell is not the enemy. The shell is where the kingdom under the dining room table lives. Our job is not to pry it open but to make a room so safe and so contained that the child chooses to come out, on her own count of three.

What to say in the car (and what not to)

Parents undo a lot of good work on the drive home, with love, by asking the wrong question. Here is the shape I coach families toward.

Not thisWhy didn't you join in? Everybody else was doing it. Next week you have to try.

ThisI saw you watching the storm game. Which part was your favorite?

Not this(At the doorway, to the teacher, over your child's head) I'm so sorry, she's really shy.

This(To your child, before you get out of the car) You can watch as long as you want tonight. Watching is part of the class. I'll be on the bench.

The difference is not politeness. It's who owns the timeline. "Next week you have to try" makes the class a test. "Watching is part of the class" makes the class a place. Shy children join places. They avoid tests.

And please stop apologizing for them. Every "sorry, she's shy" a child overhears is a small announcement that who she is right now is a problem to be managed. She's not a problem. She's a kid who likes to look before she leaps, which, let's be honest, is a trait most of us wish we had more of.

When to wait: the honest exceptions

Let me be clear, because most of what you'll read online won't say it: there are children for whom a drama class is not the right first step, and enrolling them anyway can set them back.

If your child is in real physical distress about going (not reluctance, distress: tears that last, stomachaches, sleep trouble the night before, a refusal that has held for weeks across different activities), that is anxiety, not shyness, and it deserves a conversation with your pediatrician before it deserves a drama teacher. I've written separately about stage fright and audition anxiety in young performers, and those tools help, but a drama class is not treatment, and a good teacher will tell you so.

If your child is under five, wait, or start with a short day camp instead of a weekly class. A four-year-old's shyness is usually just being four. I've written about what age a child should start theatre classes, and the short version is that five is the year the structure begins to hold.

And if you've given a class three honest weeks and your child is still on the bench with no rain in her hands, nothing moving at all, try a different room before you conclude anything about your child. Teachers are not interchangeable. The right one for a shy child is the one who is comfortable with silence, and not every theatre person is.

Closing reflection

Back to the doorway. Nora's mother, hand over her mouth, watching the wolf.

What she was really asking on that first Monday was not whether her daughter was too shy for drama class. It was whether her daughter was going to be okay in a world that rewards the loud kids. And the answer drama gives, week after week, is that there is a way to be brave that doesn't require being loud first. You borrow the courage from the wolf. You give it back when you're ready. Except you never quite give it back.

I was a kid who watched from the bench. The rooms that made me brave were the rooms with rules in them, where I could see what was coming and choose my moment. I build those rooms now. That's the whole job.

Your child is not too shy for drama class. Bring her to the doorway. Let her hold the frame. Sit on the bench. Watch for the rain.

Inspiritu,
Mr. CJ

Questions parents ask

Will drama classes help a shy child, or make it worse?

A well-run drama class helps, and the reason is specific: a shy child is rarely afraid of pretending, they are afraid of being watched as themselves. Drama hands them a character, a group, and a game to hide inside while they practice speaking up, and the courage transfers. What makes it worse is a room that asks for a solo performance in the first week, or a teacher who coaxes in front of the group. Ask how the first month is built before you enroll, and give it three classes before you judge.

How long does it take for a shy child to become comfortable in drama class?

Plan on three classes before your child does anything you would recognize as participating, and a full six to eight weeks before you see the change at home. Weeks one and two are usually watching, which is participation; week three is the first group game with a voice in it; by week six most shy children have taken a small named role and delivered a line to the room. If nothing has shifted after eight weeks, the room is wrong for your child, not the other way around, and it is fair to try a different class or teacher.

What if my child refuses to go in on the first day?

Stay, sit them next to you or on the bench, and let them watch the whole class without being asked to join. A good teacher will greet them once, offer them a job (holding a prop, being the audience), and then leave them alone. Do not negotiate at the door and do not apologize for them where they can hear it. If the second week is the same, that is still fine. If the child is in real physical distress (tears, stomachaches on the drive, refusing for weeks), that is not shyness and deserves a slower conversation with a pediatrician before a drama class.
