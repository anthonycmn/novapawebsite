Every year, from the doorway of a ballroom I helped set up, I hear a parent ask some version of the same question.

"So we have to go to Chicago in February?"

No. And the fact that so many families believe otherwise is a failure of vocabulary that our field has never bothered to fix.

One word is doing three jobs. The National Unified Auditions are a coalition of roughly twenty-five to thirty BFA programs auditioning in three cities each winter. Regional unifieds — Pittsburgh, Utah, Western New York, the one I run in Virginia — are independent events that borrowed the format and the word. And "United Auditions" is a different thing with a nearly identical name.

No single event is attended by every program. That event does not exist. But a seventeen year old and their parents, reading a school's website that says "we will be at Unifieds," have no way to know that.

I have been on both sides of those tables now, and the thing I most want to say to colleagues who run programs, coach students, or sit behind the table: our shorthand is costing families money. A family that flies to a city where two of their ten schools are auditioning has spent real money on a worse experience than a campus visit would have given them.

So I wrote the plain-English version. What the three things actually are, what happens in those five minutes, why prescreens reorder the whole year, and what the season costs line by line.

The part I'd argue with colleagues about: we tell families to count how many schools from their list will be in the building, and go only if the number is high enough. I think that's backwards. The programs a student hasn't heard of yet are the best reason to be in the room. I've lost count of how many families arrived for three schools and left committed to a fourth they met on the Saturday. You can't discover a program you've never heard of by driving to a campus you already chose.

If you work with families in this process, the vocabulary section is the part worth stealing.
