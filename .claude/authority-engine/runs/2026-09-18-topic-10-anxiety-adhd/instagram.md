## Caption

He was in three places at once. She hadn't moved since she sat down.

Two emails the morning of the second rehearsal. "He has ADHD, I'm worried he'll be a disruption." "She has anxiety, please tell me if I'm pushing her." Two apologies. Two families who had already decided their child was the problem.

Both kids were in the same room. Neither needed a different one. They needed the same room, built carefully.

Week two, I gave the boy a job: assistant stage manager for entrances. By week five he had every actor's blocking memorized and was correcting missed entrances in a stage whisper the whole cast could hear. His mother was afraid he'd be a disruption. He turned out to be the infrastructure.

The girl learned the whole score in her bedroom before rehearsals started so nothing could surprise her. By the show, she was the Gingerbread Man, and she was funny, and she knew it.

A rehearsal room does five things a classroom can't: it's predictable, it gives every body a job, being wrong is the point, feedback comes in seconds, and no one is watched alone. The anxious kid takes what she needs. The ADHD kid takes what he needs. Same room.
